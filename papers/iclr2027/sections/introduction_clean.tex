\section{Introduction}
\label{sec-intro}

Large language models give advice with apparent empathy and understanding,
yet they agree with their users more than the evidence warrants
\citep{perez2023discovering,sharma23}. In personal advice this takes a social
form, validating the person asking \citep{cheng25elephant}.

The social account of objectivity locates objectivity in organised criticism
across perspectives rather than in any single reasoner
\citep{longino1990science,popper2011open}, and a model answering one person's
account holds only that person's perspective. Narration-of-Thought (NoT), a
lightweight system prompt, asks the model to write the missing perspectives
into its reasoning as a first-person narrative of the decision-maker, the
stakeholders, the consequences of each available action and what remains
uncertain, ending in a decision (Section~\ref{sec-scaffold}), and we compare
it with standard chain-of-thought \citep[CoT,][]{wei22,kojima22}. A collective
places the perspectives in separate agents with opposed interests and sends
the cases on which they dissent to a stronger judge
(Section~\ref{sec-collective}).

We measure how often a judge model scores a response as emotionally
validating the asker, showing understanding, respect for their perspective
and care, on 150 open-ended advice questions from ELEPHANT
\citep{cheng25elephant} and seven answering models (generators) from six
vendors, three of them open-weight (Section~\ref{sec-single}).
Under claude-haiku-4-5 (haiku), the judge that scored our earlier results and
itself a generator, NoT lowers this rate on all seven, by 13 to 63 percentage
points (16 to 80 percent of the CoT rate) when their responses are
regenerated in one run under a length limit almost none reach, and by 19 to
70 points on the original responses, many of which a length limit had cut
short, up to 88 percent on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (Llama), 87 in an earlier
pass of the same judge.
With five judges each reading the full text of every original response, the
reduction holds on seven, six and five generators under haiku, gpt-4o (not a
generator) and gpt-5.4-nano, but on only one and two under Llama and
grok-4-1-fast-reasoning (grok) as judges, which rate most responses
validating either way and each reverse it on two generators
(Figure~\ref{fig:judges}).
In both sets of responses it holds under all five judges only on the Llama
generator. On claude-sonnet-4-6 (sonnet), from the haiku judge's own vendor,
no other judge finds it and in the regenerated set all four reverse it, and
on haiku's own answers only gpt-4o confirms it and the grok judge reverses it
in both sets.

The reduction comes with the scaffold's structured content as a whole, not
its narrative form. Pooled over four generators, the same five elements
written as a third-person checklist lower judged validation by 36.5 to 57.5
points under every judge, more than NoT does, while the first-person
narrative instruction alone raises it.
Neither NoT's opening persona nor, on the two generators tested and under
four of the five judges, asking CoT for NoT's length reproduces the
reduction.
Under the haiku judge no single section is shown to carry a quarter of it,
and one line asking the model to consider everyone affected raises validation.
Because the rubric scores emotional validation, part of the reduction may be
a cooler register rather than less endorsement of the asker. NoT also gives no
protection against pushback. Asked who is at fault in a dispute and then
challenged, it abandons its verdict more often than CoT (37 against 22
percent pooled over three models, mostly on Llama, with a fourth unread after
failing a quality check), and net of its instability when merely asked to
reconsider, the difference is indistinguishable from zero.
Nor does it remove the shift of its verdicts toward the asker on the two of
three models that show one.

In the collective, three copies of one model, each reasoning with NoT,
debate first-person conflict accounts as an advocate for each party and a
neutral adjudicator, a fourth copy writes the verdict, and a near-unanimous
crowd vote sets the correct one.
In descriptive comparisons its verdict is no more accurate than the same
model answering alone on five models, though two smaller earlier comparisons
point the other way, but on four of them its dissent is a sensor. On grok's
verdicts that blame exactly one party, the collective is wrong 9.8 times as
often when a second of its three agents objects as when at most one
does, against 1.5 to 6.2 times on three other models, and no difference is
detectable on haiku.
Sending the debates it flags to sonnet raises accuracy over the collective on
all five models, by 7.0 points on grok, yet descriptively the routed
collective only matches grok
answering alone, stays below Llama and DeepSeek-V4-Pro answering alone, and
on grok falls short of sonnet answering every case.
Neither result needs narration. On grok and Llama, the two models tested,
agents that reason by plain CoT make the collective more accurate than
narrating agents, by 2.0 and 4.2 points, and at least as accurate after
routing, and on grok they keep a weaker sensor (6.4 against 9.8 times) and a
smaller routing gain (5.4 against 7.0 points).

We contribute registered controls showing that the scaffold's
judge-dependent effect on judged validation, which may partly be a cooler
register, comes from its structured content and not its narrative form, a
five-judge evaluation that states every claim per judge, and evidence that
opposed agents make a poor decider but a usable sensor of their own errors, a
sensor that on grok survives without the scaffold. Every experiment run after
our registration log opened was registered in it, append-only, before its
model calls, and Appendix~\ref{app-prereg} records what was added after data
were seen, the overrun ceilings and the results that predate the log.
Unregistered analyses are labelled exploratory.
