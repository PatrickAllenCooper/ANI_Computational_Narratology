\begin{abstract}
Language models tell people what they want to hear, accept premises that
are false, and argue whatever side they are handed, and in each case the
answer has been pulled away from the evidence by something else in the
room. Sycophancy is the sharpest instance, but the problem is general, and
it survives the usual remedies because copies of one model share the same
pull and so do their votes and debates. We show that the pull can instead
be placed on purpose. A copy given a side argues it almost without
exception, which makes assignment a control rather than a cure, so we seat
two copies with opposite sides and a third with none, let them argue, and
have a moderator decide. A verdict that blames one party gives only one
advocate a reason to object, so when a second seat objects as well the case
itself is speaking, and we treat that as a flag. On such judgements the
group is wrong nearly ten times as often where the flag is raised, the flag
persists when the seats cannot read one another, and sending only the
flagged fifth of cases to a stronger judge with no side raises accuracy
over the group. The method floods where a verdict blames everyone or no
one, falls silent where the advocates never waver, and cannot make parties
cooperate where each is tempted to defect. Objectivity, where we obtained
it, was a property of how the seats were arranged and not of any one model,
and we map where that holds and where it does not.
\end{abstract}
