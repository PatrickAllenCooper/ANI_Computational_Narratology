\begin{abstract}
Language models tell people what they want to hear, accept premises that
are false, and argue whatever side they are handed, and in each case the
answer has been pulled away from the evidence. Sycophancy is the sharpest
instance, but the problem is general, and it survives the usual remedies
because copies of one model share the same pull and so do their votes and
debates. Social theory suggests where a remedy lies. On the argumentative
account of reasoning and the social account of objectivity, individuals
argue for a side and truth emerges from the structure of criticism among
them rather than from any one mind. We build that structure from copies
of a single model. A copy given a side to argue takes that side almost
without exception, so assignment is a reliable control over what a copy
will say. We use it to build a panel of three seats, each seat a copy of
the model reading the same account of a dispute under its own brief. One
seat speaks for the person who wrote the account, one for the other
party, and one for no one. The seats state and defend their positions, a
moderator with no side writes a verdict from what they said, and each
seat then accepts the verdict or objects to it. When the verdict goes
against one party, only that party's seat has a reason to object. So when
a second seat objects as well, that objection is not explained by any
assigned side and must come from the account itself, and we treat it as a
flag. Cases carrying the flag are wrong nearly ten times as often as
cases without it. The flag comes from the sides the seats were given, not
from their exchange, because it appears just as reliably when each seat
writes alone and never sees what the others wrote. And because it marks
the cases most likely to be wrong, passing only those cases, about a
fifth, to a stronger judge with no side raises the accuracy of the whole.
The method breaks down in three settings. Where a verdict blames everyone
or no one, every seat with a side has a reason to object and the flag
fires on most cases. Where the seats never depart from their sides, it
never fires. And where every party would gain from cooperating but each
is tempted to defect, no arrangement of seats changes the answer.
Objectivity, where we obtained it, came from how the seats were arranged
and not from any one model, and we map where that holds and where it does
not.
\end{abstract}
