# The game theory of advocacy and information aggregation, as it bears on the embodied sensor

Companion to `advocacy_refs.bib` (same directory, and since 2026-09-13 also at `papers/embodied_sensor/advocacy_refs.bib`). Every source below was fetched on 2026-09-12 at a primary URL and its DOI resolved against the Crossref registry, and every entry was re-fetched at a primary page on 2026-09-13 by the review, which also read the two JSTOR records (Shin, Okuno-Fujiwara et al.) that were unreachable on 2026-09-12. The fetch URL is recorded under each entry. Nothing here is cited from memory. The review of 2026-09-13 also corrected seven glosses below (Kamenica and Gentzkow, Myerson, Glazer and Rubinstein 2001, Okuno-Fujiwara et al., Shin, Dewatripont and Tirole, Gentzkow and Kamenica) where the two-sentence statement had gone beyond what the fetched text says; the corrected wording is marked. The two-sentence statement under each source gives the result as the fetched abstract or first page states it, then what it predicts for our design (two opposed interested advocates, one naive uninterested decider, dissent against interest as the credible signal, with extensions to n senders, multidimensional cases and verifiable against unverifiable claims). Where the record already tests the prediction the record item is named.

Design vocabulary used throughout, from the record. The advocates are the writer's advocate and the counterparty. The decider is the neutral seat inside the panel, and downstream the solo model that receives the flag. The signal is two or more objections in a round (the fire event). One-loser cases are YTA or NTA, both-party cases are ESH or NAH. Role-lock is the share of an advocate's verdicts fixed by its role (0.97 for grok, 0.44 for haiku).

One correction to the brief. The brief listed Glazer and Rubinstein 2001 as "Debates: a theory of persuasion" in Econometrica. No such paper exists. The 2001 debate paper is "Debates and Decisions: On a Rationale of Argumentation Rules" in Games and Economic Behavior 36(2), and the Econometrica paper is "On Optimal Rules of Persuasion" (2004). Both are verified below.

## A. Persuasion with verifiable claims, and what a naive decider needs to know

### Milgrom and Roberts (1986), Relying on the Information of Interested Parties. RAND Journal of Economics 17(1), 18-32. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://web.stanford.edu/~milgrom/publishedarticles/Relying%20on%20the%20Information%20of%20Interested%20Parties.pdf (author's PDF of the published article, first page read as an image). DOI 10.2307/2555625 confirmed at Crossref.

A strategically sophisticated and well informed decision maker can extract all relevant verifiable information from a single interested party, but when the decision maker is unsophisticated or poorly informed competition between parties whose interests are sufficiently opposed can reduce or eliminate the need for prior knowledge and for strategic sophistication. For our design this is the licence for a naive decider, since the neutral seat and the downstream solo model need no scepticism of their own provided the two advocates' interests are sufficiently opposed, and it predicts that the sensor should fail exactly where the opposition condition fails, which is the both-party regime where each advocate can object without cost to its own side (16.12 flooding, P(2+) 0.603 against 0.072, and the 16.15 within-one-loser lift of 9.83x against a both-party lift of -0.097).

### Dewatripont and Tirole (1999), Advocates. Journal of Political Economy 107(1), 1-39. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.tse-fr.eu/articles/advocates (author's institutional page, citation) and https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/baliga/ftp/Papers/dewtir.pdf (published article, first two pages read as images). DOI 10.1086/250049 confirmed at Crossref.

Organisations use competition among enfranchised advocates of special interests because advocates' rewards track their performance whereas a nonpartisan investigator pursuing several conflicting causes at once has impaired incentives, and because advocacy creates strong incentives to appeal an abusive decision, at a cost in manipulation and garbling of information. For our design this predicts that the value of the two advocates lies in what they surface rather than in what they conclude, so that a panel of identical uninterested seats should surface fewer objections even if its verdicts are as good or better, which is what 16.10 found (fire rate 0.196 for embodied seats against 0.005 for identical seats, with the identical seats more accurate). (The prediction that a second advocate for the same side duplicates cost without adding information, which an earlier draft attached here, is Krishna and Morgan's result below and not this paper's.)

### Lipman and Seppi (1995), Robust Inference in Communication Games with Partial Provability. Journal of Economic Theory 66(2), 370-405. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://sites.bu.edu/blipman/files/2021/10/robust_inference.pdf (author's PDF of the published article, first page read as an image). DOI 10.1006/jeth.1995.1046 confirmed at Crossref.

In sequential message games with an uninformed decision maker and several self-interested informed agents whose ability to prove claims is limited, robust inference rules that reach full correct inferences exist under conditions the paper characterises, and surprisingly little provability is needed when the decision maker knows only that the speakers have conflicting preferences over its action, because conflicting preferences guarantee that someone has an incentive to correct any mistaken inference. This is the closest formal statement of our mechanism, since the credible event is a correction rather than a verdict and the decider needs to know only that the two seats' interests conflict, and it predicts that the signal is an objection (the fire event carrying the +0.320 pooled error lift) while the advocates' own verdicts carry nothing (role-lock 0.97, oracle gap +0.000 [-0.004, +0.000]), that partial provability of the AITA narrative is enough, and that the guarantee lapses when the case removes the conflict, as in the both-party regime.

### Okuno-Fujiwara, Postlewaite and Suzumura (1990), Strategic Information Revelation. Review of Economic Studies 57(1), 25-47. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.ier.hit-u.ac.jp/pie/stage2/Japanese/researcher/suzumura-achievements.htm (author's institutional publication list, citation). DOI 10.2307/2297541 confirmed at Crossref (the DOI 10.2307/2298082 sometimes quoted does not resolve). On 2026-09-12 Oxford Academic and JSTOR were unreachable; the JSTOR record (Vol. 57, No. 1, Jan. 1990, pp. 25-47) was read on 2026-09-13 with its abstract.

Agents holding verifiable private information before an asymmetric-information game reveal all of it in equilibrium under sufficient conditions the paper gives, revelation being via exogenously specified certifiable statements, with examples of when revelation will and will not occur in commonly analysed games (abstract wording; an earlier draft added an unravelling mechanism through a sceptical receiver that the abstract does not state, and it is withdrawn until the section of the paper that defines the receiver's response has been read). For our design this predicts that certifiability and opposed advocates are substitutes rather than complements, in the sense that where the paper's sufficient conditions for complete revelation hold full disclosure needs no adversaries, so the adversarial structure earns its place only when those conditions fail, as when the claims are only partly provable (the design's reading of the decider as "sceptical" is its own shorthand for the receiver's equilibrium response and is not the abstract's word), which is the regime of a solo model reading a narrative, and it predicts that the sensor's contribution should shrink as the decider becomes more sceptical on its own (16.11a, a sonnet judge routing adds only +0.024 over grok solo, and the 16.15 finding that solo's own signals carry information).

### Glazer and Rubinstein (2004), On Optimal Rules of Persuasion. Econometrica 72(6), 1715-1736. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2004/11/01/optimal-rules-persuasion (publisher article page with abstract) and https://arielrubinstein.tau.ac.il/papers/70.pdf (author's PDF, header read). DOI 10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00551.x confirmed at Crossref.

A speaker who knows two aspects of a case tries to persuade a listener who can verify at most one, and the listener's optimal mechanism, which chooses which aspect to check as a function of the speaker's message and whether to accept, is simple and is found by a linear programme whose constraints come from the L-principle. For our design this predicts that a decider with a verification budget (a solo model that can re-examine one thing after a flag) should choose what to re-check as a function of what the advocates said and not of its own prior, that the choice matters most when the case has two aspects (the both-party case, where each party's fault is a separate aspect), and that a rule which reads messages literally is not optimal.

### Glazer and Rubinstein (2001), Debates and Decisions: On a Rationale of Argumentation Rules. Games and Economic Behavior 36(2), 158-173. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://arielrubinstein.tau.ac.il/papers/62.pdf (author's PDF of the published article, header and abstract read). DOI 10.1006/game.2000.0824 confirmed at Crossref.

A debate is a mechanism by which an uninformed listener extracts information from two informed debaters holding contradictory positions, and the optimal debate rules have the property that the listener's conclusion is not necessarily the literal reading of what was revealed, and the optimal rules require that the information elicited from a counterargument depend on the argument it counters (two properties the abstract states separately, not as cause and effect). For our design this predicts that the router must read an objection relative to who raised it and against what, which is the against-interest reading, and the record, not the paper, supplies the finding that the pooled anti-correlation of -0.901 is carried by which seat loses (the writer objects on 0.992 of YTA and the counterparty on 0.988 of NTA) rather than by the objection text, so a literal reader of objections would recover little.

## B. Cheap talk, from one sender to n senders and many dimensions

### Crawford and Sobel (1982), Strategic Information Transmission. Econometrica 50(6), 1431-1451. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1982/11/01/Strategic-Information-Transmission (publisher article page with abstract) and https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~vcrawfor/CrawfordSobel82EMT.pdf (author's PDF, header read as an image). DOI 10.2307/1913390 confirmed at Crossref.

With one informed sender whose preferences differ from the receiver's, every equilibrium is a partition equilibrium in which the sender reports only which interval its observation lies in, and communication is more informative the closer the two parties' preferences are, with no full revelation for any positive bias. For our design this is the transfer function of headline section 2.2, since a hard-locked advocate is a sender with a large bias whose verdict is a near-babbling constant of its role (lock 0.97, verdict information near zero) while a softly locked one (haiku, lock 0.442) is partially informative (advocate accuracy 0.818 [0.747, 0.880], prereg L4499), and it predicts that no single advocate's verdict can ever be the sensor, whatever the model.

### Krishna and Morgan (2001), A Model of Expertise. Quarterly Journal of Economics 116(2), 747-775. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/a-model-of-expertise/ (author's institutional record with abstract and DOI). DOI 10.1162/00335530151144159 confirmed at Crossref.

Two perfectly informed experts advising a decision maker add nothing over one when their biases point the same way, but when their biases are opposed consulting both is always beneficial and a sequential debate with rebuttal can achieve full revelation. For our design this predicts that opposed advocates should improve the decider's verdict over a single seat and that like-biased seats should not, and the record splits on it, since the rebuttal round is where the information appears (objections, not verdicts) while the verdict layer shows no gain (majority equals the neutral seat on 1,642 of 1,642 debates, CV oracle gap +0.000), so the Krishna and Morgan gain is realised as dissent information and not as verdict information, which is the separation of headline section 2.1.

### Battaglini (2002), Multiple Referrals and Multidimensional Cheap Talk. Econometrica 70(4), 1379-1401. VERIFIED. Preferred source for n senders.
Fetched at https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2002/07/01/Multiple-Referrals-and-Multidimensional-Cheap-Talk (publisher article page with abstract) and http://www.mbattaglini.com/multiple-referrals-and-multidimensional-cheap-talk (author's page). DOI 10.1111/1468-0262.00336 confirmed at Crossref.

When the state is multidimensional and there is more than one sender, full revelation in every state is generically possible even when the conflict of interest is arbitrarily large, and what matters is the local behaviour of the senders' indifference curves at the receiver's ideal point rather than the proximity of ideal points. For our design this predicts that the number of advocates should match the dimension of the case and that their interests must not be collinear, so two exactly opposed advocates span a one-dimensional case (one loser) and cannot span a two-dimensional one (each party's fault judged separately), which is the both-party regime where the signal floods, and it predicts that an n-perspective extension should choose perspectives whose interests point in independent directions of the case rather than adding more seats along the same axis.

## C. Mechanisms, mediation and adversarial procedure

### Myerson (1986), Multistage Games with Communication. Econometrica 54(2), 323-358. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/1986/03/01/multistage-games-communication (publisher article page with abstract). DOI 10.2307/1913154 confirmed at Crossref.

Any multistage game with a communication mechanism run by a central mediator has its equilibria characterised as communication equilibria, refined to sequential communication equilibria that never use codominated actions, and predominant equilibria obtained by iterated elimination exist. For our design this predicts that any outcome a mediator achieves is achievable by a direct mechanism with the same commitment, so the value of a moderator lies in the mechanism's order and form rather than in a mediating intelligence. The paper does not say that a mediator adds no information (in its model the mediator receives private reports and issues recommendations), so the record's 16.13d finding that the mediator seat adds nothing over a naive reader is consistent with this reading and is not predicted by it; an earlier draft's "a mediator adds coordination and not information" was the author's gloss and is withdrawn.

### Shin (1998), Adversarial and Inquisitorial Procedures in Arbitration. RAND Journal of Economics 29(2), 378-405. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/economics/Papers/abstracts/shina.htm (author's institutional abstract page, working-paper version of December 1996) on 2026-09-12, with publication data from Crossref, DOI 10.2307/2555894. JSTOR record read 2026-09-13: The RAND Journal of Economics, Vol. 29, No. 2 (Summer, 1998), pp. 378-405 (28 pages), published abstract read there, which resolves the page-range caveat the 2026-09-12 pass carried.

Even when the arbitrator is on average as well informed as the two opposing parties, the adversarial procedure in which the parties make their cases to an impartial arbitrator is strictly superior to the inquisitorial procedure in which the arbitrator adjudicates on the basis of his own investigations, a superiority that stems from the adversarial procedure's ability to allocate the burden of proof effectively and thereby extract the maximal informational content from apparently inconclusive contests (published abstract). The December 1996 working-paper abstract adds that there are increasing marginal returns to improvements in the information of an interested party; that sentence is not in the published abstract and is attributed to the working paper only. For our design this predicts, unconditionally on any property of the model, that opposed advocates arguing to a neutral seat should beat an equally capable neutral seat investigating alone, and that the mechanism depends on a well-defined burden of proof, which exists in a one-loser case (the accused party's advocate must rebut) and does not in a both-party case where no default is clean. The record splits on the unconditional prediction. On the verdict layer grok contradicts it (the embodied collective's S2 0.843 sits below the neutral seat's 0.860 and grok solo's 0.911) and haiku agrees with it (S2 0.893 beats every rule over its seats' verdicts, `aggregation_rules.json`, unregistered, 187 debates over 100 items). On the dissent layer the one-loser against both-party split (9.83x against -0.097) matches the burden-of-proof clause. The lock-conditional reading in the design document (adversarial beats inquisitorial only where lock is soft) is an observation from one model and not a prediction of Shin.

## D. Bayesian persuasion, one sender and competing senders

### Kamenica and Gentzkow (2011), Bayesian Persuasion. American Economic Review 101(6), 2590-2615. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.101.6.2590 (publisher article page with abstract). DOI 10.1257/aer.101.6.2590 confirmed at Crossref.

A sender who commits to a signal structure before seeing the state benefits from persuasion if and only if the concave closure of the sender's expected payoff, taken as a function of the receiver's posterior belief, exceeds that payoff at the prior (Corollary 2, read from the AER text on 2026-09-13; an earlier draft misplaced the condition on the receiver's value of information, which the paper does not state), and the paper characterises the optimal signal, with applications to litigators, lobbyists and salespeople. For our design this predicts that a single advocate can move a naive decider who takes a threshold action (at fault or not) even without lying, so a decider that is the audience of one advocate's case is exposed, and it predicts that reading the collision between two advocates rather than either one's argument is what protects the decider, which is the reason the sensor is placed on the edge between the advocates and not on any advocate's output.

### Gentzkow and Kamenica (2017), Competition in Persuasion. Review of Economic Studies 84(1), 300-322. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://economics.stanford.edu/publications/competition-persuasion (author's institutional page, citation) and https://www.nber.org/papers/w17436 (working-paper page recording the published version and carrying the abstract). DOI 10.1093/restud/rdw052 confirmed at Crossref.

With several senders who choose what to communicate, competition does not in general increase the information revealed, and a single condition on the information environment (the set of signals each sender can generate) is necessary and sufficient for equilibrium outcomes to be no less informative than collusion regardless of preferences, and the same condition governs whether adding senders or decreasing the alignment of their preferences necessarily increases information. For our design this predicts that adding an n-th advocate is guaranteed to raise information only when each advocate can unilaterally refine what the others reveal (without that condition an added sender can still raise information in particular cases, since the condition governs when the increase is necessary for all preferences), so more seats along the same axis add nothing (same-model x3 aggregation gain about 0.01, cross-vendor x3 about 0.04, error correlation 0.6 to 0.7 against 0.33 in `aggregation_passthrough.json`), and it predicts that an n-perspective design should be judged by whether each added perspective can produce a verifiable refinement the others cannot suppress.

## E. Voting and aggregation with strategic voters

### Austen-Smith and Banks (1996), Information Aggregation, Rationality, and the Condorcet Jury Theorem. American Political Science Review 90(1), 34-45. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/information-aggregation-rationality-and-the-condorcet-jury-theorem/26AF80760F07CCB9E1E4F604C6811CF6 (publisher article page with abstract). DOI 10.2307/2082796 confirmed at Crossref.

Sincere voting by all jurors is not a Nash equilibrium even when they share a common preference, because a rational juror conditions on being pivotal, so the Condorcet claim that majorities do better than individuals lacks a rational-choice foundation. For our design this predicts that the three seats' verdicts cannot be read as sincere independent signals and that a majority over them is not an aggregation, which the record confirms since the majority equals the neutral seat on every debate and the effective voter count is one, and it predicts that any n-seat extension must establish conditional independence and sincerity before the jury theorem can be invoked, which the measured error correlation of 0.6 to 0.7 among same-model seats rules out.

### Feddersen and Pesendorfer (1998), Convicting the Innocent: The Inferiority of Unanimous Jury Verdicts under Strategic Voting. American Political Science Review 92(1), 23-35. VERIFIED.
Fetched at https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/convicting-the-innocent-the-inferiority-of-unanimous-jury-verdicts-under-strategic-voting/2464A10255F23A692DFDC6B92E46E1A9 (publisher article page with abstract). DOI 10.2307/2585926 confirmed at Crossref.

Under a unanimity rule strategic jurors vote as if pivotal, which can raise the probability of both kinds of error, and the probability of convicting an innocent defendant can increase with jury size, whereas majority and other non-unanimous rules produce lower error. For our design this predicts that the fire threshold should not be unanimity (all seats object) and that a threshold of two of three is the better rule, that adding seats under a unanimity trigger can make the sensor worse rather than better, and more generally that the trigger rule must be chosen with the seats' strategic conditioning in mind rather than read off from an independence assumption.

## F. Coalitions among n interested seats (optional)

### Chwe (1994), Farsighted Coalitional Stability. Journal of Economic Theory 63(2), 299-325. VERIFIED.
Fetched at http://chwe.net/michael/lcs.pdf (author's PDF of the published article, first page read as an image). DOI 10.1006/jeth.1994.1044 confirmed at Crossref.

The largest consistent set is a solution concept for situations in which coalitions form freely without binding contracts and are farsighted, in that a coalition considers that after it acts another may react and a third react in turn, and it is nonempty under weak conditions and applies to strategic-form games and majority voting. For our design this predicts that once there are more than two interested seats the dissent edge is no longer mechanical, because the pairwise fact that one committal verdict undermines exactly one of two opposed stakes (headline section 2.3) does not extend to three or more stakes where sub-coalitions can object together without cost to any member, so an n-advocate design must be checked for coalition-proof dissent rather than assumed to inherit the two-seat anti-correlation.

## What the set says together, for the design document

- Two opposed interested advocates before a naive decider is the Milgrom and Roberts, Lipman and Seppi, Shin and Dewatripont and Tirole configuration. All four say the decider needs only to know that the interests conflict, that the credible event is a correction against interest, and that the value lies in what is surfaced rather than in what the advocates conclude. The record's role-lock 0.97, oracle gap +0.000, fire-rate contrast 0.196 against 0.005, and within-one-loser lift 9.83x are the instances. Shin's unconditional verdict-layer prediction (adversarial beats inquisitorial) is contradicted on grok and met on haiku, and the set does not predict that split.
- The regime where it fails is predicted by the same theory. Milgrom and Roberts need sufficiently opposed interests, Lipman and Seppi need conflicting preferences, Shin needs an allocable burden of proof, and Battaglini needs the senders to span the case's dimension. The both-party case violates all four at once, and the record's flooding (P(2+) 0.603, lift -0.097) is the instance.
- For n perspectives the two relevant theorems point the same way. Battaglini says match the number of senders to the dimension of the case and keep their interests non-collinear. Gentzkow and Kamenica say adding senders is guaranteed to help only when each can unilaterally refine the others' disclosure. Chwe says that beyond two interested seats coalition-proofness must be checked. Feddersen and Pesendorfer say the trigger threshold must not be unanimity.
- For the verdict layer, Austen-Smith and Banks, Crawford and Sobel and Krishna and Morgan together predict what the record measured, that the advocates' verdicts are near-babbling, that a majority over strategic seats is not an aggregation, and that the gain from opposed experts arrives through rebuttal (dissent) and not through the vote.
- For the reader of the signal, Glazer and Rubinstein (2001 and 2004) predict that the flag must be read relative to who raised it and against what, and that a decider with a verification budget should choose what to re-check as a function of the messages, which is a design of its own that the router has only partly explored (verdict-type router in 16.15).
- Myerson predicts that a mediator achieves nothing a direct mechanism with the same commitment cannot, so the moderator's value is in the order and form of messages. 16.13d's finding that the mediator seat adds nothing over a naive reader is consistent with that and is not derived from it.
